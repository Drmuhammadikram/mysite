      title: 'Four measures that are routinely confused'
      subtitle: ''
      text: |-
        This is the dataset where terminology does the most damage, so it is worth being precise.

        **Deforestation** is the permanent conversion of forest to another land use — cropland, pasture, settlement. It is what most people mean when they use the word.

        **Net change in forest area** is deforestation minus afforestation. A country can be planting faster than it clears, showing net gain while deforestation continues. Indonesia is a live example: net forest area increased in the most recent reporting, while deforestation continued at over 100,000 hectares a year.

        **Tree cover loss** is satellite-observed and counts every loss of tree canopy, including commercial harvest, fire, and storm damage — much of which regrows. It is consistently larger than deforestation and measures something genuinely different.

        **Deforestation rate** expresses loss as a share of remaining forest. A country with little forest left can have a small absolute loss and an alarming rate.

        A headline citing one of these while implying another is the single commonest error in forest reporting.

      title: 'How to read it'
      subtitle: ''
      text: |-
        **Net gain does not mean no deforestation.** Compare the net change and deforestation maps for the same country. Plantation forestry and natural forest are counted together in the FAO figures, which means monoculture planting can offset primary forest loss in the statistics while replacing very little of its ecological function.

        **FAO data is country-reported.** Countries submit their own figures to the Global Forest Resources Assessment. Definitions of forest vary, and reporting capacity varies more.

        **Satellite data is independent but measures canopy, not forest.** Global Forest Watch sees what is there from above. It cannot distinguish a clear-cut plantation from a cleared rainforest.

        **Deforestation figures come in five-year blocks.** The FAO assessment reports averages over assessment periods, so those maps show period values rather than single years. The hover panel shows the reference year where it differs from the one selected.
