      title: 'What the accounts measure'
      subtitle: ''
      text: |-
        The Ecological Footprint converts human demand on nature into a single unit: the global hectare, a hectare of land or sea with world-average biological productivity. Biocapacity converts ecosystems' regenerative capacity into the same unit.

        Expressing both in the same currency allows a direct comparison. Where footprint exceeds biocapacity, a country runs an ecological deficit, met either by importing, by depleting its own stocks, or by using the global commons as a carbon sink. Where biocapacity exceeds footprint, it holds a reserve.

        The accounts cover six land types — cropland, grazing land, forest, fishing grounds, built-up land, and the forest area required to absorb carbon emissions. That last component dominates: for most industrialised countries, the carbon footprint is over half the total.

      title: 'How to read it'
      subtitle: ''
      text: |-
        **Per-person and total tell different stories.** A country with a small population and modest per-person footprint may still hold a large reserve. Use the metric buttons above the map to switch.

        **Deficit is not the same as harm.** A country may run a deficit sustainably by importing from countries with reserves. What matters globally is the aggregate: humanity's total footprint exceeds Earth's biocapacity, and no amount of trade resolves that.

        **The carbon component drives most deficits.** For most high-income countries, reducing the footprint means reducing emissions rather than changing land use.

        **National averages conceal regional variation.** A country with adequate biocapacity overall may have regions in severe deficit.
